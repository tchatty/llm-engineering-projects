## Executive Summary

This Watershed white paper proposes a standardized framework for corporate-level accounting of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from the use of third-party AI services (employee productivity assistants, direct access to LLMs, and AI features embedded in enterprise software). Data center electricity demand is expected to grow from roughly 5% of U.S. consumption in 2025 to 9-17% by 2030, and no widely accepted corporate methodology exists. Published per-query estimates differ by several orders of magnitude depending on system boundary, provider, electricity-use assumptions and grid mix. The framework aims to be defensible with current data, tiered, transparent, updatable as provider disclosure matures, and built for action rather than disclosure alone.

The framework has three elements. (1) A comprehensive system boundary: inference compute, training, data center overhead (PUE), host system capacity (CPU, memory, NIC), idle and reserved capacity, and amortized embodied emissions of accelerators and supporting infrastructure, with user-side devices out of scope. (2) A functional unit of kgCO2e per million tokens (kgCO2e/MTok) for inference, reported alongside electricity consumption (kWh), so customers can pursue efficiency and clean-electricity matching; tokens are also what companies already track for cost. (3) A three-tier calculation: the Spend Tier (spend times an economic factor, default 0.134 kgCO2e per 2023 U.S. dollar, BEA sector 518200), the Activity Tier (token volumes times modeled energy intensity, PUE and grid intensity; hyperscaler defaults 0.32 Wh per 1,000 input tokens and 0.96 Wh per 1,000 output tokens, PUE 1.10), and the Provider Tier (provider-reported per-token figures, the most precise). Location-based and market-based Scope 2 figures should both be reported; training emissions are reported as a separate line item.

In a stylized example, FinCo LLC spends $100,000 per year on a frontier model API (17,600 MTok). The same use yields 13.4 tCO2e (Spend Tier), 9.1 tCO2e (simplified Activity Tier), 5.4 tCO2e location-based and 3.7 tCO2e market-based (detailed Activity Tier), and 4.5 / 3.2 tCO2e (Provider Tier, using Google's Gemini Apps figures as a proxy). Amortized training contributes 32-56% of the footprint under a central assumption of 10^14 lifetime tokens and 10,000 tCO2e training emissions (5-83% across 10^13 to 10^15 tokens); embodied hardware contributes 4-11%.

Key data gaps are on the provider side: only Google publishes per-prompt figures (0.24 Wh and 0.03 gCO2e for the median Gemini Apps text prompt), and non-production benchmarks can overstate inference energy 4-20 times. The paper proposes a prioritized disclosure table (Table 5) with per-model electricity intensities per token, grid factors, embodied and training emissions, and overhead. For customers, management levers are decarbonizing electricity, prompt design, model routing (reasoning models can be about 30 times more energy intensive), region selection (U.S. subregions vary more than five-fold) and data center selection. The paper also discusses unresolved issues (training amortization, distillation attribution, Scope 2 methods, behind-the-meter generation) and broader concerns (air quality, water, grid expansion, electricity prices).

## Summary (Main Points of the Paper)

### The problem
Electricity demand from data centers is expected to grow from roughly 5% of U.S. consumption in 2025 to between 9% and 17% by 2030, and corporate artificial intelligence (AI) use is following a similar trajectory, spanning employee productivity assistants, direct access to large language models (LLMs), and AI features embedded in enterprise software. AI emissions today are a small share of footprints for many enterprises, but that share is unlikely to remain small for long. Without reasonable estimates, companies cannot set reduction targets or identify effective decarbonization levers as emissions grow. Companies, regulators, and auditors are asking for emissions estimates that withstand scrutiny, but no widely accepted methodology exists today. Published per-query estimates can differ by several orders of magnitude depending on what is counted, which provider is measured, and what assumptions are made about electricity use and the grid mix.

### The proposal
This white paper proposes a standardized framework for corporate-level AI emissions accounting. The framework is designed to be defensible with current data constraints, tiered to meet companies where their data are, transparent about its assumptions, updatable as provider disclosure matures, and built for action rather than disclosure alone. Since AI emissions accounting is still nascent, it has the opportunity to design for actionability from the outset, so that measurement incentivizes responsible choices during AI's rapid buildout.

### Three elements of the framework
1. The system boundary is designed to be comprehensive, including inference compute, training, data center overhead, host system capacity, idle and reserved capacity, and amortized embodied emissions of accelerator hardware and supporting infrastructure.
2. The functional unit is kilograms of CO2e per million tokens for inference, reported alongside electricity consumption so that customers can consider strategies for reducing emissions and matching clean electricity. Tokens are also the metric that many companies track for cost reasons, which allows token-level emissions reporting to leverage engineering efforts.
3. The three-tier calculation approach runs from the Spend Tier (a spend-based backstop) to the Activity Tier (activity-based estimates using token volumes and modeled energy intensity) to the Provider Tier (provider-reported per-token figures, which is the most precise tier). Reaching the most detailed accounting tiers requires provider disclosures that largely do not yet exist, especially for frontier models. Even where per-token figures are published, they tend to be fleet-wide estimates for a typical prompt rather than values linked to specific customer models, regions, or traffic.

### Stylized example: four-fold range of estimates
For a company with $100,000 annual spending on a frontier model, accessed programmatically for internal productivity tools and a customer-facing chatbot, the same AI use yields 13.4 tCO2e under the Spend Tier, 3.7-5.4 tCO2e under the Activity Tier, and 3.2-4.5 tCO2e under the Provider Tier. Spend-based methods can overstate or understate emissions depending on context. In this example, the premium-priced frontier API implies less electricity per dollar than the sector average, producing an overestimate, but the direction could reverse if, for instance, providers subsidize usage or operate on more carbon-intensive grids. Training emissions contribute between 32% and 56% of the footprint depending on assumptions about total lifetime token volume, which no frontier model currently discloses. For widely deployed models, however, the more actionable data gap is inference: operational electricity is likely a larger share of total emissions across the lifetime of heavily used models and is the component most directly addressable through clean electricity procurement, region selection, and provider efficiency improvements.

### Disclosure and provider self-interest
The accuracy of estimates depends on parameters from model providers and their cloud hosts. The framework suggests a standardized disclosure table to close gaps for frontier models, including per-model electricity intensities per token, grid emissions factors, embodied and training emissions, and data center overhead. Data transparency may be in providers' commercial self-interest: when better data are available, AI's footprint has typically been materially lower than published estimates suggest, as non-production benchmarks can overstate inference energy by 4 to 20 times by omitting efficiency gains from deployment at scale (batching, caching, hardware co-design optimizations that are proprietary and therefore absent from external estimates; Oviedo, et al., Joule 2026, which focuses on open-weight models). A provider that discloses figures lowers emissions its customers report and prevents its products from being misrepresented by third-party estimates.

### Management levers
For customers, the framework suggests practical levers through two channels. Prompt design and model selection reduce electricity consumed per task (reasoning models can be 30 times more energy intensive than smaller variants), while region selection and clean energy matching reduce the carbon intensity of each kWh consumed (U.S. subregions vary by more than five-fold in carbon intensity). Reporting electricity alongside emissions keeps both channels visible and lets companies attribute year-over-year changes to efficiency gains rather than grid decarbonization or vice versa. The framework is designed to establish a methodology, data flows, and provider relationships that will be valuable as AI emissions grow and to demystify AI's footprint with auditable data before inflated narratives harden into convention. The emphasis is on accounting that drives action (efficiency, model choice, region, clean electricity matching) in addition to reporting.

### Getting Started in Four Steps
1. Inventory your AI use by channel. List vendors and how each is accessed (e.g., hosted assistants, embedded features) and pull AI-related data.
2. Estimate at the tier your data supports. Use the Spend Tier as a backstop for everything, and move channels where you have token volumes to the Activity Tier.
3. Ask providers for better data. Request the essential fields in Table 5, starting with per-token electricity intensity and serving region, and upgrade to the Provider Tier where disclosed.
4. Report both metrics and act on the levers. Report emissions alongside electricity, then apply the management levers: model routing, prompt design, region selection, and clean electricity matching.

## Introduction

Corporate sustainability teams, investors, and regulators are increasingly asking how to account for greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from AI use by employees and enterprise tools, and finding that no widely accepted methodology exists. These questions reflect burgeoning AI and data center demand, which is expected to consume 9% to 17% of U.S. electricity by 2030, up from ~5% in 2025 (EPRI, 2026, "Powering Intelligence," Report 3002034696). Data centers serve a wide variety of workloads beyond AI, including streaming, cloud storage, and enterprise software; AI represents a growing share of total data center electricity, hence the focus of this framework.

Estimates in the literature have inconsistent system boundaries, varying energy intensity factors, different grid emissions methods, and omit embodied emissions, idle capacity, and other factors that materially alter environmental impacts.

### Opposing trends
Several dynamic trends are driving AI emissions in opposing directions. Algorithmic efficiency for a given level of performance has roughly halved every eight months (Ho, et al., 2024), and inference-side optimizations (batching, KV and prompt caching, speculative decoding, quantized inference) and optimized software and hardware co-design can dramatically reduce per-query energy. At the same time, AI use is shifting toward higher per-task compute intensity: reasoning models generating extended inference; multi-modal systems handling text, image, audio, and video; and agentic systems executing multi-step workflows autonomously, so that a single request triggers many model calls.

### Objectives of the paper
- Establish a framework for estimating the electricity consumption and GHG footprint of corporate AI use, including empirically grounded data and broad system boundaries to accurately reflect environmental impacts, encourage data gathering, and incentivize actions to reduce emissions.
- Apply this framework to produce illustrative estimates using the best-available public information.
- Highlight key data gaps for customers calculating their Scope 3 footprint from AI services. Rapid changes to models, use cases, and grids mean the framework should be updated frequently.
- Design the accounting to support action. The framework treats disclosure and decision-usefulness as complementary and reports electricity alongside emissions, so that measurement points toward concrete levers for reducing impact.

### Scope definition
"AI emissions" primarily refers to GHG emissions attributable to a company's use of third-party AI services, including employee productivity assistants, direct access to LLMs, and AI features embedded in enterprise software. Contracted AI use is primarily classified as Scope 3, Category 1 (Purchased Goods and Services) for many companies, whether the service is accessed directly from the model provider or through a cloud intermediary. Emissions from training or self-hosting a company's own models are accounted for directly through that company's own compute and are outside this paper's scope.

## Why Is Measuring AI Emissions Hard?

### 1. Limited data from first-party providers
Accurate AI emissions accounting is constrained by the tension between the need for transparency and the legitimate commercial sensitivity of providers. Many parameters most valuable for emissions accounting (model-specific inference energy, training energy, batch sizes, hardware utilization) overlap with information providers have reasonable business interests in protecting. This is especially true of efficiency gains: because the most recent hardware configurations, software optimizations, and serving infrastructure improvements are proprietary, external estimates based on publicly available benchmarks tend to lag actual performance (Masanet, et al., Joule 2024).

Emissions estimates from public data can therefore considerably overstate the footprint of frontier models. Oviedo, et al. (2026) find that benchmarks overstate inference energy by 4 to 20 times by failing to account for efficiency gains realized only at production scale, including batching, KV caching, speculative decoding, and optimized hardware co-design. A standardized disclosure framework could narrow this gap, even one that protects the most commercially sensitive details.

A related challenge is the quickly changing landscape of AI model architectures and user behavior (Figure 1). Alternative assumptions (data center efficiency, hardware choice, grid carbon intensity) create orders-of-magnitude variation in emissions for equivalent workloads. Footnote on the Jevons paradox: efficiency improvements can make technologies cheaper and more accessible, driving greater adoption. AI's low per-query cost unlocks new use cases (multi-agent workflows for customer support, automated image and video generation, coding assistance). Even if a simple query has a similar energy intensity to a web-based search, total consumption can increase many-fold due to a proliferation of use cases that were not economically viable.

### 2. System boundary ambiguity and disagreement
Typical LLM queries run through many layers with different energy and emissions impacts. Published per-query numbers can differ by several orders of magnitude depending on what is counted in terms of embodied hardware, training, and inference. There is also no agreed-upon functional unit for emissions comparison; generative AI outputs (per query, per token, per image) vary widely in computational intensity.

### 3. Heterogeneity in AI use cases
Enterprise AI use is heterogeneous: prompt complexity, modality, token length, and degree of agentic or reasoning-intensive computation all vary substantially within and across companies. Each dimension can shift energy consumption by orders of magnitude relative to a simple chat query (Figure 2). These challenges are compounded by providers that do not isolate AI-specific compute from general cloud use.

### 4. Training and embodied emissions are large but poorly allocated
Model training and manufacturing accelerator hardware generate large upfront carbon costs. These must be amortized over time, but no standard convention exists for spreading them across use and model lifetimes. Multi-tenant infrastructure further complicates allocation, since data centers may serve thousands of users and models simultaneously. The commercial sensitivity of the denominators compounds this: the total number of tokens a model will serve over its lifetime, duration of a training run, and hardware refresh schedules are all data providers have business reasons to treat as confidential.

For companies with AI use concentrated in widely deployed frontier models, where lifetime token volumes make amortized training relatively small, inference electricity is likely the more material and tractable near-term data gap. Training disclosure remains important for boundary completeness and for models with limited deployment, but the commercial sensitivity of training-related data and the maturity of clean energy procurement tools point toward prioritizing inference-side data collection first (see Table 5).

### 5. Electricity accounting is complicated by clean resource procurement and behind-the-meter resources
Many AI providers use power purchase agreements (PPAs) and renewable electricity certificates (RECs). These instruments can reduce market-based emissions even when the local grid is carbon-intensive, potentially producing a large gap between location- and market-based estimates. This raises questions about hourly and geographic matching of clean energy to AI workloads and about dynamic load balancing. Multiple data centers can serve models depending on the user's location, network latency, and capacity, making it exceedingly difficult to trace the grid mix for a given query.

The increased prevalence of behind-the-meter (BTM) generation and energy storage at data centers (power produced on-site without passing through the grid) also complicates electricity accounting, which standard factors do not capture. Roughly 30% of planned U.S. data center capacity plans to build BTM generation, with nearly 75% powered by natural gas (Cleanview, 2025, "Bypassing the Grid"). Because this generation is consumed on-site, it is not reflected in grid-average location-based emissions factors, and its treatment under Scope 2 is unsettled: on-site combustion that the operator owns is conventionally Scope 1, while contractually arranged BTM sits in market-based Scope 2 with no established convention for passing the resulting intensity to enterprise customers.

### Overall implication
Estimating GHG emissions from enterprise AI use goes beyond simply applying cloud-accounting methods to a larger workload. The combination of limited first-party data, unstandardized system boundaries, heterogeneous use cases, opaque provider infrastructure, and contested electricity accounting creates uncertainty. Each input carries its own error bar, leading estimates to vary by orders of magnitude. The stakes are rising because AI emissions could represent a larger share of corporate footprints within the planning horizon; as that share grows, spend-based approaches become increasingly inadequate, and customers, regulators, and auditors will expect emissions figures that withstand scrutiny. At the same time, AI investment is becoming a considerable driver of new transmission, firm low-emitting generation, and clean energy procurement, and a framework that tracks electricity use alongside emissions gives companies and providers data to show that AI demand can contribute to grid decarbonization.

The methodology is designed to be defensible within this constraint, tiered to meet companies where their data are, transparent about assumptions, and built to evolve with greater data availability and model updates. It is also designed for action: building the methodology around levers companies can pull is a deliberate choice to keep measurement and mitigation linked as AI capabilities scale.

## Literature Review

### Overview
This section synthesizes peer-reviewed literature, industry publications, and the current state of disclosures. In general, there are two approaches to AI emissions estimates: model-based (assumptions about hardware specifications, model parameters, and usage patterns such as token lengths) and empirical measurements (standardized hardware and tasks). Many papers provide incomplete coverage and narrow measurement boundaries (Figure 3).

### Training-focused studies
- Strubell, et al. (2019), "Energy and Policy Considerations for Deep Learning in NLP": focus on model training, including variation across models.
- Patterson, et al. (2021), "Carbon Emissions and Large Neural Network Training": emissions for model training are 88 times lower than the Strubell, et al. estimate once Google-specific measurements for data center efficiency, hardware choice, and grid carbon intensity are taken into account.
- Luccioni, et al. (2022), "Estimating the Carbon Footprint of BLOOM, a 176B Parameter Language Model": a comprehensive study showing embodied emissions roughly double the training-only emissions estimate.

Caveat: model-based estimates likely do not reflect efficiencies from frontier models deployed at scale. For instance, Jegham, et al. (2025) estimate roughly 1.6-2.2 Wh for a medium Gemini prompt, an order of magnitude above the 0.24 Wh median reported in Elsworth, et al. (2025). Also, fine-tuned small language models (SLMs) and task-specific models can have much lower energy intensity per task than general-purpose LLMs for well-defined use cases. The framework focuses on frontier models because enterprise GHG accounting is largely concentrated in off-the-shelf API consumption from major providers; companies with material SLM deployments should apply the same tiered methodology using measured or vendor-reported energy intensity.

Specific energy and emissions intensities depend on usage patterns (prompt complexity, token length) and AI characteristics (number of parameters, latency to first token, tokens per second, batch size), all of which change frequently by model. Earlier studies did not use models near the Pareto frontier of performance efficiency, and the use of open models for benchmarking may also alter estimates. Oviedo, et al. (2026) provide direct evidence of this gap: non-production benchmarks overstate inference energy by 4-20 times relative to production deployments, primarily because open benchmarks assume batch sizes of one and omit optimizations hyperscalers apply at scale, producing a systematic upward bias.

Note on AI Energy Score: common benchmarks such as AI Energy Score have limitations relative to frontier models at scale. It assumes a batch size of one for all models, which overestimates footprints. It also reports GPU plus host CPU energy only and excludes facility overhead, which can increase the figure by 25% to 2x depending on data center configuration, with frontier hyperscalers at the lower end of the range.

### Areas of emerging consensus
- Inference can dominate lifetime emissions for many models, especially as reasoning and agentic models become more prevalent. Agentic and reasoning workflows (multi-step model chains, tool use, self-reflection loops) can multiply per-task emissions by orders of magnitude relative to a single-turn query, while variation across input and output modalities (text, image, audio, video) adds further spread.
- Geographical factors create extensive variation in carbon intensity due to differences in regional electricity generation mixes.
- Embodied hardware emissions are smaller than model training and inference operational (electricity) emissions.

### Outstanding challenges and areas of active disagreement

#### Conceptual: training emissions amortization and attribution
Why training belongs in the boundary: from a Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) perspective, a trained model is a form of capital good, a large upfront investment of compute that produces a durable asset generating output over a service life. Capital goods are included in the inventory boundary unless proven negligible (ISO 14040 and ISO 14044), and the illustrative results below confirm that for frontier models this threshold is clearly met. This is analogous to physical hardware, whose embodied emissions the GHG Protocol considers capital emissions; standard practice capitalizes these and amortizes across an asset's productive life. Model training has a similar rationale of spreading emissions across the output the model serves over its deployed life, not attributing them to a single query, customer, or reporting period. The unsettled questions are how to scope the training workload and how to select the amortization value.

Amortization and attribution are important unsettled questions, for both the numerator (which activities count as training) and the denominator (how to spread a known quantity over a model's deployed life). A one-time training run can emit thousands of tons of GHG emissions (though disclosures are limited for frontier models; Meta's Llama 3.3 70B model card is one of the few public estimates, with total location-based GHG emissions of 11,390 tCO2e). Different approaches produce materially different results, which is particularly fraught when models are deprecated mid-life. For example, when GPT-3.5 was superseded by GPT-4, GPT-3.5's training emissions should be amortized over its actual deployed life, however brief, rather than the longer service life originally anticipated; mid-life deprecation raises the per-token training intensity of the superseded model. Training and embodied hardware emissions likely warrant different treatment, given different expected service lifetimes. AWS applies linear (time-based) depreciation to hardware, while Luccioni, et al. use a six-year life for BLOOM's CNRS infrastructure, though frontier models are reported to refresh hardware on shorter cycles. In either approach, time is a proxy for the more fundamental allocation basis: the share of a hardware unit's functional output (accelerator-hours, energy throughput, tokens processed) consumed by a given workload. Where functional-output data are unavailable, time-based depreciation is a reasonable fallback, but it can misallocate embodied emissions if utilization varies materially over the asset's life.

Numerator (training-side workload boundary): the final training run is only one component of a model's broader development compute. As pre-training, mid-training, post-training, and continuous-learning phases become larger shares of compute, "training" is shorthand for the larger set of model development workloads. Broader upstream development includes ablation studies, hyperparameter searches, experiments on smaller-scale versions, synthetic data generation, fine-tuning, training runs never deployed, and iterative reward-model training via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Epoch AI's analysis of IPO filings finds that final training runs account for a minority of total R&D compute (Denain and Wu, March 23, 2026). Denominator: the total number of tokens a model will serve over its lifetime remains commercially sensitive and unverifiable from outside the provider.

Distillation: a separate attribution problem arises where a large "teacher" model generates outputs used as training data for a "student" model. This is a co-product allocation problem in LCA terms, specifically a form of open-loop recycling, where an output of one product system (the teacher's inference compute) becomes an input to another (the student's training). The teacher produces two outputs, a deployed model and a training subsidy that enables the student to be trained with less compute, raising the question of whether the student should partially inherit the teacher's training emissions. A defensible resolution draws on LCA for recycled inputs: the teacher's inference compute during distillation is a measurable input to the student's training (analogous to electricity or hardware depreciation), but the teacher's training emissions remain attributed to the teacher alone. This is similar to recycled aluminum in manufacturing LCA, where reprocessing energy for the recycled input is counted but the original smelter's emissions are not reallocated to the downstream product. A desirable property is that distillation is incentivized as an emissions reduction approach, due to the student's lower training compute (ISO 14044 and ISO 14067 contain extensive provisions on allocation for open-loop recycling, the basis for this treatment). Near-term estimates will likely undercount training emissions from frontier models, because the upstream multiplier from failed runs and experiments is omitted without provider disclosure, while efficiency gains from distillation are observable in lower training compute. This bias should be flagged in disclosures.

Interim approach: report training emissions as a separate line item rather than rolling them into a per-token operational figure. This preserves transparency and avoids locking in an amortization or scope convention before the field reaches consensus. The recommendation is aimed at model developers disclosing their training emissions (as Meta does for Llama); customers would include that separate figure in their inventory rather than reconstruct it and, absent provider disclosure, would rely on the Activity Tier default.

#### Conceptual: accounting for differences in user behavior
Variation in how employees and customers use AI can alter a company's aggregate footprint. Output sequence length appears to be among the most reliable proxies for per-query energy consumption, and the spread across query types is large. The Hugging Face AI Energy Score finds that reasoning models consume roughly 30 times more energy on average than standard inference, with worst-case queries reaching 113 times the baseline. Agentic workflows compound this, because a single request can trigger anywhere from 5-50+ model calls through tool use, self-reflection loops, and multi-step reasoning chains; emissions attributed to one "interaction" may understate true compute by an order of magnitude or more. The distribution of energy per prompt is likely skewed, with a small number of complex queries accounting for a disproportionate share of consumption (Elsworth, et al. note outlier queries vary "significantly over time" in Gemini serving). Characterizing and communicating that distribution in a corporate inventory (mean, median, or percentile-based range) is itself an open question.

#### Conceptual: accounting for electricity emissions
Electricity emissions accounting for AI inherits unresolved Scope 2 debates and amplifies them. The central question is whether market-based accounting (using energy attribute certificates, RECs, or PPAs) should be credited against a company's emissions, or whether location-based accounting is more appropriate. This is not specific to AI, but the scale of AI providers' clean energy procurement and the opacity of their serving infrastructure raise the stakes. Definitions: location-based emissions use the average grid intensity where consumption occurs; market-based emissions reflect a company's contractual electricity choices and apply the Scope 2 Quality Criteria hierarchy from GHG Protocol Scope 2 Guidance based on contractual instruments; Energy Attribute Certificates (EACs), including Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs), are tradable instruments that reduce market-based Scope 2 emissions when retired against consumption.

The GHG Protocol's Scope 2 dual-reporting guidance nominally applies, requiring location- and market-based figures, but no convention exists for how a model provider passes either figure to an enterprise customer in a way auditably linked to the customer's usage. Behind-the-meter generation, often natural-gas-fired and increasingly common as a bridge to power, typically falls outside standard grid-mix reporting.

Choice of emissions factor: most academic studies use average grid intensity because it is the most available and aligns with GHG Protocol location-based accounting, but some specifications permit long-run marginal rates, which tell a different story about consequential impacts of AI demand. Long-run marginal emissions rates reflect the intensity of new generation built or avoided in response to sustained changes in electricity demand (rather than the average mix of the existing grid or a supplier's contracted mix); they map to the intuition that data center load growth is influencing capacity decisions (Gagnon, et al., PNAS 2022). They may be more theoretically appropriate for large and growing loads but are rarely used and methodologically contested. The proposed GHG Protocol Scope 2 revision adds uncertainty, as it may alter the rules governing EAC crediting.

Recommendation: the most defensible near-term approach is to follow existing GHG Protocol Scope 2 guidance and report both location- and market-based figures. A long-run marginal rate could be reported as an optional figure for companies wishing to signal a more forward-looking view of grid impacts. Although AI emissions sit in Scope 3 for most customers (where the GHG Protocol does not yet formally endorse market-based accounting), the framework recommends dual reporting on the rationale that market-based figures capture supplier procurement decisions and create an incentive for upstream reductions. The 2015 GHG Protocol Scope 2 Guidance introduced dual reporting and has not been formally extended to upstream categories, though ongoing Scope 3 revisions could move in this direction. A credible market-based figure also requires that clean electricity claims be allocated across a provider's workloads in proportion to consumption rather than selectively assigned to specific products, which could drive a workload's reported emissions toward zero without procuring additional low-emitting resources; providers reporting per-workload market-based factors should disclose the allocation basis.

#### Data: obtaining first-party data from AI model providers
Data is the binding constraint on accurate accounting, and the pace of change in the underlying systems makes this a moving target. Energy consumption scales directly with accelerator utilization, but inference stacks are evolving rapidly in ways that cut in both directions. As capabilities advance, the average query may become more compute-intensive as customers shift more work to AI. Other impacts are ambiguous: adaptive model routing (a provider selects a model based on query complexity) means customers may not know which model served their request, let alone its energy intensity; mixture-of-experts architectures, which activate only a subset of parameters, add further heterogeneity.

## Proposed Framework for Estimating Emissions from AI Use

A defensible and usable framework needs several elements: (1) an appropriate system boundary for AI energy and emissions measurements; (2) a common unit of measure tied to a transparent allocation rule; (3) a tiered model of measurement that meets companies where data are and reflects data availability. The framework is designed to accept better data as providers publish it and to be updated as models change.

### Element 1: System Boundary

Without a consistent system boundary, emissions estimates are less comparable. Boundary choices alone can drive order-of-magnitude variation across published figures (Figure 3). Narrower approaches that count only active accelerator energy understate AI's footprint relative to ones that include host system overhead, idle capacity, embodied hardware, or training.

The proposed boundary is designed to be comprehensive and comparable across providers (Figure 5). Consistent with the GHG Protocol, it covers the full upstream and downstream life cycle of AI service delivery, from hardware manufacturing through inference. It is consistent with the guiding principles of the GHG Protocol Corporate Accounting and Reporting Standard and with ICT-sector guidance that "all data center emissions should be allocated to the services that the data center delivers," interpreted to encompass Scopes 1, 2, and 3 across the full AI service life cycle.

| Component | In scope? | Notes |
|---|---|---|

End-of-life impacts are excluded from OCP's boundary, but can be included in embodied hardware or reported separately; end-of-life estimates are currently limited, but including them can help incentivize recycling and other mitigation. |
| Training emissions | Yes, amortized | Reported as a separate line item rather than rolled into the per-token operational figure; amortization basis disclosed (see Outstanding Challenges) |

#### Energy boundary and workload boundary
A complete boundary has two dimensions. The energy boundary tracks the energy resources for a given unit of compute (Table 1). The workload boundary includes the computational steps counted as part of the service. An inference request may invoke not only the foundational model call but also safety and moderation classifiers, routing to a serving model, and output guardrails. The question is more consequential on the training side, where research, pre-training, and distillation workloads must be scoped. The workload boundary is less settled than the energy boundary, but the practical takeaway is that a comprehensive energy boundary drawn only on the headline model call will understate the footprint, especially with safety tooling, routing, and agentic orchestration.

Figure 5 depicts the system boundary for the AI emissions framework.

#### Embodied emissions and training in the boundary
Embodied emissions from AI accelerator hardware and data center construction are separate boundary decisions. Scope 3 embodied carbon is sometimes called "capital emissions" (similar to financial amortization of capital expenditures) and can include IT hardware, buildings, and other supporting equipment. Amortization refers to normalizing non-operational emissions outside the use phase across an asset's service life so the full carbon cost is allocated to customers. AWS assumes linear depreciation, a six-year service life for server racks, a 50-year service life for buildings, and includes cradle-to-gate emissions of server racks (Amazon, 2025, "AWS Customer Carbon Footprint Methodology"). The Open Compute Project's work on hardware life-cycle accounting (OCP, 2025, "Embodied Carbon Disclosure Base Specification v1") provides the most developed framework and is the reference point for embodied emissions. Vendors generally treat amortization as a downstream issue for customers, so the burden of allocating embodied carbon to workloads falls on the method rather than the provider.

Boundary questions for training-related emissions include not only amortized emissions from the primary training run, but also how to treat failed runs, hyperparameter searches, fine-tuning, and other upstream development activities.

#### Operational boundary
The boundary should include inference compute and the full serving stack to deliver inference to users. A figure counting only active AI accelerator energy will be lower than one that includes active CPU and DRAM energy, data center overhead captured through PUE (cooling, power conversion, supporting infrastructure), and idle machine energy. Serving infrastructure is provisioned for peak demand, so GPUs run with lower capacity factors to ensure availability and low latency. Google's methodology includes idle capacity, while many other studies do not, which accounts for part of the spread in the literature. (This component moves in the opposite direction from the production-efficiency adjustments discussed earlier; the net direction of any figure depends on which effects dominate.)

### Element 2: Functional Unit

The functional unit determines how emissions are normalized and compared across providers, models, and use cases. Kilograms of CO2 equivalent per million tokens (kgCO2e/MTok) is a natural candidate for inference workloads, because tokens are the most common billing and logging metric tracked by major providers and roughly scale with electricity use and emissions. This makes the unit familiar to enterprise customers and straightforward to populate from existing usage data.

- Input and output tokens should be tracked and reported separately wherever possible, because they are not energetically equivalent: input tokens are consumed in a parallelized prefill pass, while output tokens are generated sequentially in a more energy-intensive decode pass. A blended unit is a defensible fallback when the split is unavailable, but it will misstate emissions for workloads whose input/output mix departs from the assumed ratio.
- Limitation: tokenizers vary across models and versions, so the same input text leads to different token counts and token volumes are not fully fungible across providers. In practice this variation is typically smaller than other uncertainties (training emissions, grid intensity), and the token remains the most tractable unit for enterprise accounting.
- AI cost management is maturing as a corporate discipline (minimizing input length, routing tasks to cheaper models, caching aggressively), and dollars saved through token reduction also reduce emissions. For companies maximizing output per token, the business case for addressing emissions aligns with this operational metric.
- The per-token unit can accommodate evolving billing conventions. As providers introduce differentiated pricing for cached input tokens, partial-rerun decode, or similar features, the methodology can apply a provider-disclosed factor specific to that token category without changing the framework.
- A volume-based unit treats tokens as fungible units of value, but tokens expended correlate only loosely with task quality, so a volume-only metric could under-penalize verbose, low-value generation. A future unit might normalize by delivered value (e.g., emissions per completed task), but there is currently no auditable measure of output quality.

#### Functional unit for training and fine-tuning
GPU-hours may be a more appropriate unit of account for training, reflecting how compute is priced and tracked (in contrast, cloud emissions accounting typically normalizes on resource-time units: vCPU-hour for compute, GB-hour for memory, GB-month for persistent storage residency, GB transferred for network egress). The most disaggregated form, exemplified by Meta's per-release Llama disclosures, reports GPU-hours alongside hardware type and resulting tCO2e on both location- and market-based bases, allowing customers to recompute under alternative grid or amortization assumptions (Meta's Llama 3.3 model card: 11,390 tCO2e location-based and 0 tCO2e market-based alongside 7.0M GPU hours). That approach also requires an amortization denominator (e.g., total tokens), which may expose commercially sensitive information about scale and depreciation. Another form embeds training as an amortized per-token contribution, a ratio that plugs into customer calculations without providing numerator or denominator explicitly. The intent is to lower the bar to first-party disclosure while preserving transparency.

#### Electricity and emissions as co-equal outputs
The framework treats electricity consumption and GHG emissions as two co-equal outputs, both normalized to the per-token functional unit. Tracking electricity unlocks additional management levers, including clean electricity matching against AI workloads, time- or location-shifted serving toward lower-emitting grids, and tracking of efficiency improvements. Emissions and electricity are linked through the customer's grid factor, and separate reporting preserves transparency about which lever (efficiency gains or grid decarbonization) is driving annual footprint changes. This reflects a fundamental design principle: accounting should be built for action.

### Element 3: Multi-Tier Calculation Approach

The framework's data quality hierarchy has three tiers with different levels of input data. Companies should use the highest tier for which reliable data are available, expecting that data availability will improve over time.

#### Spend Tier
When data availability is limited, emissions can be approximated by multiplying total AI vendor spend for the reporting period by an economic emissions intensity. AI-related spend is mapped to the closest available BEA sector code and multiplied by an emissions factor from a supply-use input-output model. The current default is 0.134 kgCO2e per 2023 U.S. dollar (CEDA, BEA sector 518200, Data Processing, Hosting, & Related Services). Open CEDA is offered free under a CC BY-SA license (openceda.org). As an environmentally-extended input-output factor, this value embeds the full upstream supply chain, including embodied emissions from hardware manufacturing and data center construction (much of it reflecting imported production, e.g., overseas semiconductor manufacturing), so the Spend Tier captures embodied emissions in a single factor. Other resources such as USEEIO (maintained by the Cornerstone collaboration between Watershed, ERG, and Stanford) have similar factors for the BEA data processing category. The Spend Tier is the starting point for companies without activity-level data.

Limitations:
- The BEA 518200 factor reflects the average intensity of the data processing and hosting sector, which includes low-margin workloads such as web hosting and managed databases. It does not capture the economics of AI inference, where providers charge a premium per token relative to the energy consumed. Spend-based factors can therefore misestimate true AI emissions by several times in either direction, depending on context. As a complement, cost-per-activity benchmarks (e.g., the ARC-AGI leaderboard, which reports model-specific inference costs in dollars per million tokens) can cross-check implied energy intensities and whether a provider's pricing is consistent with the sector-average assumption.
- Data vintage: underlying input-output tables are often two to five years old, so the factor may not reflect the most recent supply chain structure.
- A more tailored factor could be built by estimating emissions per dollar of AI service from observed or modeled energy use, emissions intensity, and frontier-model pricing, but it would still not be company-, model-, region-, or time-specific and would mask variation in hardware and workload characteristics. Although activity-based approaches and provider data are preferable, such factors would improve on generic data-processing factors because they reflect the distinctive workloads and pricing of AI.

Spend Tier results should be treated as provisional placeholders, flagged as such in disclosures, and replaced with Activity or Provider Tier data when token-level data become available.

#### Activity Tier
When provider-reported figures are unavailable, electricity and emissions are estimated from token volumes using these equations:

- Electricity (kWh) = (I x EI_in + O x EI_out) x PUE
- Operational/embodied emissions (kgCO2e) = Electricity x GI / 10^3 + E_emb x (I + O)
- Amortized training emissions (kgCO2e, reported separately) = E_train x (I + O)

where I and O are input and output token volumes (where separate volumes are unavailable, a blended energy-intensity factor can be applied to total volume), EI_in and EI_out are energy-intensity factors for the prefill and decode phases respectively, PUE is the power usage effectiveness, GI is the grid carbon intensity, and E_emb is amortized embodied emissions. Amortized training emissions are reported as a distinct line item so that the term (which carries the highest uncertainty) does not obscure the rest. The 10^3 divisor converts gCO2e to kgCO2e.

Caveats on constant per-token factors: they are a reasonable approximation for dense models. Mixture-of-experts architectures activate only a subset of parameters per token, so factors derived from total parameter counts or dense-model benchmarks may overstate their energy use. Constant per-token factors also assume per-token energy is independent of prompt shape; in practice prefill cost grows super-linearly with input length, decode energy per output token rises with context length, and cached input is cheaper, so a single recombinable pair will tend to undercount long-context and agentic workloads. Where providers expose cache-hit token counts, the prefill term can be disaggregated to reflect the lower energy intensity of cached input (which reuses key-value states computed in a prior request); this matters for repeat-context workloads such as agentic loops, where a blended factor would overstate electricity.

Default values are proposed for each parameter, presented for feedback; final defaults will be published alongside the next version of the framework.

#### Provider Tier
The most precise approach is to pull kgCO2e/MTok directly from the AI provider's disclosure or a machine-readable feed. This is preferred wherever provider data exist, because it embeds the actual energy intensity, grid mix, and embodied hardware allocation for the specific infrastructure serving the customer's workload without external assumptions. Today, Google is the only frontier-model provider to publish per-prompt figures at this granularity (Elsworth, et al., 2025), but its disclosure reports a fleet-wide median for Gemini Apps text prompts that cannot be linked to a specific customer's traffic, model, or region. The convening process described later in the paper is designed both to extend equivalent disclosure to other providers and to surface the company-specific telemetry customers would need to populate a true Provider Tier estimate.

Provider Tier data sourced through a cloud intermediary (e.g., Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, Bedrock, Azure OpenAI): an important consideration is whether the reported figure isolates AI-specific compute from the provider's broader cloud workload, which hyperscalers handle unevenly. When figures arrive as aggregate cloud emissions (AI and non-AI combined), an allocation step is needed. It can be usage-based (cycles, tokens, accelerator-hours) or revenue-based (share of revenue from AI services); the appropriate choice is often company-specific. Even where AI emissions are isolated, cloud carbon reports remain monthly rollups rather than per-token figures; combining them with customer-side telemetry brings cloud-mediated AI traffic closer to Provider Tier estimates. Examples: AWS's Sustainability Console and Microsoft's Emissions Impact Dashboard provide per-service breakdowns but do not yet distinguish Bedrock or Azure OpenAI as separate lines in their published methodology; Google Cloud's Carbon Footprint methodology applies a single uniform allocation across all covered products but does not define an AI-specific allocation step.

## Data Needs and Provider Disclosures

### Customer-side telemetry
Inventories need data on both sides: emissions factors from providers and customer activity data. Activity-based data availability is uneven across channels and providers. Channels of corporate AI consumption fall into five categories:
1. Hosted chat and productivity assistants: vendor-operated chat interfaces and productivity assistants (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude.ai, Gemini app, Microsoft 365 Copilot). Telemetry is accessible via the vendor's admin console or API, though token-level data are uneven and often hidden behind credits or interaction counts.
2. Hosted developer tools: IDE-embedded or command-line coding assistants (e.g., Cursor, Codex, Claude Code, GitHub Copilot). Token-level data are mixed across vendors.
3. First-party LLM API: customer applications calling the model vendor's API directly with an organization-scoped key. Token, cache, and model data are provided but with limited regional information.
4. Cloud-mediated LLM API: calls through a hyperscaler (e.g., Azure OpenAI in AI Foundry, AWS Bedrock, Vertex AI). Telemetry lives in the hyperscaler's monitoring stack. Serving region is an explicit dimension, though the model vendor has no view into this traffic.
5. Self-hosted deployments: customer-operated inference of downloadable-weights models (e.g., Llama, Mistral). The company holds whatever telemetry it chooses to instrument.

Figure 8 maps these channels (rows) against data dimensions that drive emissions calculations (columns). Customers with material first-party-API or cloud-mediated-API usage can usually populate Activity Tier calculations directly from existing logs. Customers whose AI footprint is concentrated in hosted chat or developer tools often have limited or no visibility into token or credit usage; for them the Spend Tier is the starting point until more granular information is available. (When providers do not charge based on usage, AI activity may be a smaller cost and emissions contributor relative to the supplier's broader footprint, so the precision gains from the Activity Tier may not justify reconstructing token volumes from indirect signals.) Telemetry availability is a per-channel concern as much as a per-provider one: the same model accessed through three channels can yield three different qualities of input data.

### Provider disclosures
The largest constraint on accurate AI emissions accounting is limited first-party data from companies operating the infrastructure. Until providers publish data in standardized and auditable forms, frameworks rely on assumptions entailing order-of-magnitude uncertainty.

Early academic estimates appear to have overstated AI emissions substantially. Patterson, et al. (2021) find that Google-specific values for data center hardware and grid carbon intensity lower training emissions 88-fold. Elsworth, et al. (2025) estimate a 47-fold reduction in electricity consumption per prompt for Gemini year over year. Where AI's true footprint is lower than headlines suggest, standardized disclosure is the most credible way to say so; transparency serves providers' interest by replacing inflated external estimates, which are attributed to their customers' footprints, with auditable figures. The Stanford Foundation Model Transparency Index (December 2025) finds that the vast majority of frontier models provide no meaningful environmental or energy-related information.

For many enterprise customers using widely deployed models, inference electricity is likely the higher-priority disclosure for three reasons. First, for models with larger user bases, lifetime tokens are large enough that amortized training emissions are modest relative to inference (the 32-56% training share in the example reflects an assumed lifetime token volume at the lower end of the Figure 7 range). Second, inference electricity connects directly with actionable levers (clean electricity procurement, region selection, provider efficiency) that are unavailable for sunk training costs. Third, training-related data are perceived as commercially sensitive, while per-token inference energy intensity may be more tractable to disclose. Training disclosure remains important for boundary completeness and for models with limited deployment.

### State of provider transparency
Existing disclosures are highly uneven, with an overall lack of frontier-model data for granular customer-level accounting.
- Meta is the most transparent among model developers on training emissions, publishing GPU hours, hardware type, and tCO2e for each Llama release on location- and market-based bases (excluding non-GPU server consumption, cooling, embodied hardware, and amortized training). Llama 3.3: 11,390 tCO2e location-based versus effectively zero market-based, illustrating the gap dual reporting can reveal.
- Google provides the most transparency on the operational side, publishing per-prompt energy and emissions for Gemini Apps (Elsworth, et al., 2025). Google Cloud's Carbon Footprint tool gives Scope 1, 2, and 3 breakdowns by project, region, and service, but training emissions are not included.
- AWS launched a Sustainability Console in April 2026 providing Scope 1-3 emissions by service and region via API, independently verified by Apex, but without a per-token breakout for inference.
- Microsoft provides corporate reporting and an Emissions Impact Dashboard for Azure customers but no AI-specific breakout for services such as Azure OpenAI.

### Standards landscape
More than a dozen working groups claim to be developing AI emissions standards, but none had been widely adopted as of writing. The Green Software Foundation's SCI for AI specification, ratified in 2025, extends the ISO/IEC 21031:2024 Software Carbon Intensity standard to AI systems, defining a carbon intensity rate and a framework for calculating and comparing emissions across AI deployments; it is customer-facing rather than provider-facing (it describes how a customer should structure accounting, not what a provider must disclose). The GHG Protocol's ongoing Scope 3 revision (2024 through 2026) is consequential: its Standards Working Group has proposed software carbon-intensity specifications that would formally cover cloud and AI services under Category 1 (Purchased Goods and Services). The proposed method is designed to be consistent with the direction of both efforts. Under the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), providers of general-purpose AI models must maintain technical documentation including a breakdown of the model's energy consumption (Article 53 and Annex XI), estimated from computational resources where measured data are unavailable; energy consumption is also one factor that can contribute to classification as posing "systemic risk," which carries further obligations. Buyer-side coalitions (e.g., the Business Council on Climate Change (BC3) AI Sustainability Vendor Engagement Resources) are also articulating shared disclosure expectations, signaling demand-side pressure.

## Conclusions and Next Steps

### Practical approaches to reducing AI-related emissions
AI emissions may currently be a small fraction of footprints for many companies, but converging trends increase expectations for growth. The framework aims to establish methods, data, and provider relationships that will be increasingly important, to demystify AI's footprint and to embed emissions accounting into AI procurement and engineering decisions. A practical advantage of the per-token unit is that it aligns with how companies already optimize AI spend: cost and emissions efficiency share a common metric, so token-level reporting piggybacks on existing work. Beyond accounting, the framework supports a small set of levers that differ in effort and impact.

Highest-leverage lever: decarbonizing data center electricity. Most of the variation in AI emissions across providers and regions relates to the carbon intensity of electricity consumed. Grid decarbonization and credible clean electricity procurement by data center operators (co-located renewables and energy storage, hourly matched PPAs, contributions to transmission and firm low-emitting generation) can reduce emissions across all workloads without behavioral changes from users (EPRI, 2026). Although largely outside enterprise customers' direct control, the framework supports it: standardized energy intensity disclosure lets customers select lower-emitting providers and regions, creating market demand for providers investing in cleaner infrastructure.

The remaining levers reduce electricity consumed per task rather than the carbon intensity of each kWh. They require added effort to track and implement, with potential tradeoffs with productivity and other goals:
- Optimize the prompt: the cheapest reductions come before a request is sent, including writing effective prompts, trimming context windows, eliminating redundant calls, and avoiding agentic loops that add no value. Output sequence length is among the most reliable proxies for per-query energy, and some use cases may over-generate relative to the user's need.
- Choose the right model for the task: frontier reasoning models can be roughly 30 times more energy-intensive than smaller production models for equivalent queries. Task-aware deployment (routing simple requests to smaller models and reserving frontier capability for when it adds value) is the highest-impact lever available to many customers, though competitive pressures on capability work against it. Unlike most Scope 3 categories (where a primary customer lever is spend reduction), AI emissions are better addressed through smarter consumption; spend reduction is a poor proxy, because it could drive customers toward cheaper, less efficient models or self-hosted deployments with worse emissions profiles and no disclosure. Routing potentially aligns customer cost savings, provider utilization, and emissions reduction, a rare case where commercial and environmental incentives point the same way.
- Choose the right region: where customers can select serving regions, lower-carbon grids reduce emissions. eGRID subregions in the U.S. and EEA country factors in Europe vary by more than five-fold across available regions.
- Choose the right data center in a region: where technical capabilities allow, select data centers with credible clean electricity matching and higher efficiencies. This requires disclosure that is not yet standard but is part of the framework's requests for providers.

### Broader context
AI emissions accounting is part of broader public and policy concerns about data center growth, which a defensible framework must acknowledge even where they fall outside the strict GHG inventory.
- Local air quality: AI infrastructure concentrates load geographically, which can produce criteria pollutant exposure (NOx, PM2.5, SO2), particularly where backup generation runs on natural gas or diesel. Carbon-only accounting misses these distributional impacts.
- Water use: AI workloads can be water-intensive at the cooling stage or entail withdrawals and consumption for electricity generation. The WUE row in Table 5 is a starting point tracking on-site water use. Electricity figures could be used to calculate upstream (off-site) water use from power generation, but this is less tractable than the carbon analogue: (1) water accounting requires distinguishing withdrawal (diverted and largely returned) from consumption (evaporated or otherwise removed), which respond differently to cooling technology and carry different local impacts; (2) water intensity varies substantially within a single generation type depending on cooling system (once-through, recirculating, dry), ambient climate, and water source, unlike heat rates and carbon intensities, which are comparatively uniform across plants of the same fuel. A regional water factor would carry wider uncertainty, and regionally resolved withdrawal and consumption data are not published at the spatial granularity available for location-based grid carbon factors.
- Behind-the-meter generation: Cleanview (2025) documents growth of BTM generation co-located with new data center capacity, especially gas-fired. It is unclear whether these arrangements meet long-term reliability requirements or function as a temporary "bridge to power" until grid connection is possible, and their treatment under GHG Protocol Scope 2 remains contested.
- Grid expansion and modernization: AI demand is an important potential near-term driver of transmission investment, low-emitting firm generation, and demand flexibility. Beyond direct electricity demand, AI could influence economy-wide emissions through effects on innovation, technology costs, and energy system efficiency. Recent modeling using expert elicitations (Beath, et al., 2026) suggests AI-induced CO2 changes by 2040 range from roughly +11% above to 1.4% below a baseline without AI, depending on the scale of AI deployment and the extent of policy intervention, reflecting uncertainty about whether AI's productivity effects (including potential reductions in the cost of renewables, carbon capture, and nuclear) will outpace its electricity demand. The framework focuses on direct emissions from corporate AI use, but companies and policymakers should weigh this broader context when interpreting per-query or per-company figures in isolation.
- Electricity prices and consumer concerns: rising data center demand has become a salient issue in regional rate cases and political debates. Data centers can play constructive roles through tariff design, transparent reporting, flexibility commitments, and potentially lower electricity prices (EPRI, 2026, "Win-Win Watts"; Wiser, et al., 2025). Recent research (Watten, et al., 2026) estimates, using an instrumental variables approach, that data centers caused average retail electricity rates to fall modestly in the U.S. from 2015 to 2024.
